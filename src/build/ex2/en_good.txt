## Opening
What if forgiveness doesn't mean forgetting what they did to you? Many believers carry a guilt the Bible never asked them to carry. Today we'll look at what the Bible says about forgiveness, and by the end of this episode you'll have three clear truths for forgiving without pretending nothing happened.

I'm Juan. Every week we open the Bible slowly, verse by verse. Let's go straight to the text.

## First truth: forgiveness starts with what God did
[TEXTO: Matthew 18:21-22]
First, let's go to a passage where Peter asks a very practical question. Matthew 18:21-22 says: "Then came Peter to him, and said, Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times? Jesus saith unto him, I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven."

Peter thought he was being generous. Some Jewish teachers of his day taught that forgiving three times was enough. Peter doubled that number and added one more. Jesus answered with a number nobody can keep track of in a notebook. The point isn't to count to four hundred ninety. The point is to stop counting.

[B-ROLL: ancient market, coins on a table]
Right after that, Jesus tells the parable of the unforgiving servant, in Matthew 18:23-35. A king forgives his servant a debt of ten thousand talents. That was more money than a worker could earn in a lifetime. That same servant walks outside and runs into a coworker who owed him a hundred denarii, about a hundred days of wages. He squeezes him without mercy and ends up throwing him in prison.

The lesson is direct. Whoever understands how much God has forgiven them learns to forgive others. That kind of forgiveness doesn't come from willpower. It comes from remembering the cross. When we forget the size of our own debt, the other person's offense looks enormous. When we remember it, the offense shrinks to its real size.

But here's what almost nobody notices in this passage. The servant isn't punished for his debt. He's punished for refusing to forgive after he'd been forgiven.

## Second truth: forgiving isn't forgetting
Second, forgiving is not the same as forgetting. Think about Joseph. His brothers sold him as a slave and went years without hearing from him. When they finally meet again in Egypt, Joseph has the power to get even. In Genesis 50:20 Joseph tells his brothers that they planned evil against him, but God turned it into good.

Joseph didn't pretend nothing had happened. He named the harm clearly: he told them they had planned to hurt him. And still he chose not to hit back. That's biblical forgiveness: seeing the whole wound and still giving up the right to collect on it.

Forgiving means canceling the debt, not erasing the memory. You can forgive someone and set healthy boundaries at the same time. Forgiveness is given by grace. Trust, on the other hand, is rebuilt over time and through actions.

Picture a woman whose friend betrayed her by sharing a secret. She can forgive today, from the heart, and still wait before trusting her with something personal again. That isn't a lack of forgiveness. It's wisdom.

Has anyone ever told you that if you really forgave, you should act like nothing happened? That idea sounds spiritual, but Scripture doesn't teach it.

[TEXTO: Luke 23:34]
Look at the highest example of all. On the cross, Jesus prayed for the people crucifying him. Luke 23:34 records it this way: "Then said Jesus, Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do." Jesus didn't say what they were doing was okay. He called sin what it was. But he didn't hold on to the debt to collect it later. He placed it in the Father's hands.

That's the model. This kind of forgiveness doesn't deny injustice. It hands injustice over to God, who judges rightly, so you don't have to carry it.

## Third truth: forgiveness sets you free first
[TEXTO: Ephesians 4:32]
Finally, forgiveness frees the one who forgives before anyone else. Ephesians 4:32 says: "And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you."

Notice the word "even as." We forgive the same way God forgave us: without waiting for the other person to deserve it first. God didn't wait for us to be good before sending his Son.

Holding a grudge is like carrying a rock so you can throw it at someone someday. While you carry it, you're the one bearing the weight. The other person goes on with their life. You get tired, you harden, and you lose your peace.

Some people have spent ten or twenty years collecting the same debt. They replay it at night. They retell it at every family dinner. Without realizing it, the offense becomes the center of their story.

Jesus taught something similar in the Lord's Prayer. In Matthew 6:12 he teaches us to ask God to forgive our debts as we forgive our debtors. Every time we pray those words, we tie two realities together: the forgiveness we receive and the forgiveness we give. They can't be separated.

This doesn't mean God's forgiveness depends on our performance. It means a heart that has truly received grace starts to resemble the one who gave it. Forgiving is a sign that the gospel has taken root.

Is there someone you're still making pay for a debt God already forgave you?

[PAUSA]
Here's an exercise for this week. Write that person's name on a piece of paper. Underneath, write down honestly what they did to you. Then pray, read Ephesians 4:32 out loud, and hand that debt over to God. You don't have to feel anything special in that moment. Forgiveness is a decision before it's a feeling. The feelings usually come later.

## Closing
