How many times have you said the Lord's Prayer without hearing a single word you were saying? Today you'll discover what we ask for in each line of the prayer Jesus taught. And by the end you'll have a way to pray it every morning without empty words.

Let's pray. Father, teach us to pray the way you taught your own. Amen.

## A prayer we repeat without thinking
[TEXTO: Luke 11:1]
It all starts with a request from the disciples. Luke 11:1 says: "And it came to pass, that, as he was praying in a certain place, when he ceased, one of his disciples said unto him, Lord, teach us to pray, as John also taught his disciples."

Notice what they didn't ask for. They didn't ask him to teach them to preach, or to do miracles, or to argue with the Pharisees. They asked him to teach them to pray. They had watched Jesus pray and something in that prayer was unlike anything they knew. Jesus didn't give them a lecture on prayer. He gave them a prayer. Short. Six petitions that fit in half a minute.

[B-ROLL: hands folded, sunrise over a small town]
That's the first problem we have. That prayer became so familiar we stopped hearing it. We say it at weddings, at funerals, at church, and the mouth runs ahead of the heart. Jesus warned against exactly that two verses before teaching it.

[TEXTO: Matthew 6:7]
Matthew 6:7 says: "But when ye pray, use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking." It's almost ironic: the prayer Jesus gave so we wouldn't repeat empty words became the one we repeat most emptily.

There are two versions of this prayer in the Gospels. Matthew's, in the Sermon on the Mount, is the long one everybody knows. Luke's is shorter: it opens with just Father and has five petitions instead of six. That tells us something important. Jesus probably taught it more than once, with slightly different words. He wasn't dictating a magic formula where every syllable counts. He was teaching a pattern. And a pattern can be used in your own words.

Today we're going to read it line by line, with Matthew 6:9-13 as our base, and we're going to discover it isn't a prayer to recite. It's a map. Each line is a territory of life Jesus wants us to bring before the Father.

## Our Father which art in heaven
[TEXTO: Matthew 6:9]
We start in Matthew 6:9: "After this manner therefore pray ye: Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name."

The first word already changes everything: Father. In the Old Testament God is called Father of the people a handful of times, and always in a collective sense. No Israelite approached God in personal prayer calling him Father. Jesus did, always, and now he tells his disciples: you too. That was scandalous. It's as if someone introduced you to the president of your country and said: call him Dad.

What does it mean to pray to a Father and not to a judge or a king? It means you start from relationship, not from merit. A son doesn't earn the right to talk to his father. He has it by being a son. If you've ever gone quiet before God because you felt unworthy to speak to him, this word is for you. Jesus commands you to start with Father.

Paul understood this so well he wrote it twice, in Romans 8 and Galatians 4. He says the Spirit makes us cry Abba, Father. Abba is the word a small child used for his dad in Aramaic. Paul, the Pharisee trained by the best teachers, says the sign that the Spirit lives in you is that calling God that way comes naturally. Not with a title. With a child's word.

But notice the second word: our. It isn't my Father. Jesus teaches us to pray in the plural even when we're alone in our room. You never pray alone. When you say our Father, you're praying alongside the persecuted believer in another country, the widow in your church, the brother you're fighting with. It's impossible to pray the Lord's Prayer seriously and despise another child of the same Father.

Think about what that does to pride. If I pray my Father, I can believe I'm special. If I pray our Father, I've just admitted that the person I can't stand in the congregation has exactly the same access I do. That one word disarms more divisions than many sermons on unity.

And then: which art in heaven. This isn't a mailing address. It's a reminder of who the Father is that you're speaking to. He is close, Father, but he isn't your equal; he is in heaven. He has all authority and all resources. An earthly father can love his child deeply and have nothing to help him with. This Father doesn't have that problem.

Those two truths go together and need each other. If you only have Father, you can turn him into a soft friend who agrees with you about everything. If you only have in heaven, you can turn him into a distant power you're afraid of. Jesus joins the two in a single line: near and mighty, intimate and holy. So the first line already locates you. You're speaking to someone who loves you as a child, who joins you to all his other children, and who can do anything. Start like that, and the rest of the prayer changes its tone.

Which of the two is harder for you? Believing he is Father, or remembering he is in heaven? Keep that in mind, because the answer tells you which part of this prayer you need most.

## Hallowed be thy name
Then comes the first petition. Notice that the first petition isn't for us. It's for God: hallowed be thy name.

Hallowed means treated as holy, set apart, put in the place it deserves. The name, in the Bible, is the person himself, his reputation, his character. When Moses asks God his name at the burning bush, he isn't asking for a label; he's asking who are you. So the petition is: Father, may your name be treated as what it is, in my life and in the world.

Why does Jesus start there? Because the order of the prayer reveals the order of the heart. If we're honest, we would start with bread, or with forgiveness, or with the problem keeping us up at night. Jesus starts with the Father's glory. Not because our needs don't matter, but because when God's name is in its place, everything else finds its place.

[B-ROLL: a small church, people leaving, a neighborhood street]
And how is God's name hallowed in practice? Ezekiel gives us a clue.

[TEXTO: Ezekiel 36:22-23]
In Ezekiel 36:22-23 God tells his people: "I do not this for your sakes, O house of Israel, but for mine holy name's sake, which ye have profaned among the heathen, whither ye went. And I will sanctify my great name, which was profaned among the heathen." In other words, God's name is hallowed or profaned in the lives of those who carry it. The nations looked at Israel and drew conclusions about the God of Israel. Asking hallowed be thy name is asking that your life not be an argument against God. You're asking that your neighbor, watching you, think better of God and not worse.

There's an illustration that helps me. If a child carries his father's last name and misbehaves at school, people don't say what a bad kid. They say: look how he was raised. The father's name carries what the son does. We carry the name of Christians, the name of Christ. What we do in traffic, at work, online, lands on him.

That's an uncomfortable petition. Ask it anyway. Jesus put it first. And notice it's a petition, not a promise you make. You don't say: I will hallow your name. You say: may your name be hallowed. You ask God to do it, in you too. That's a relief, because if it depended only on your effort, you'd already know how it ends.

## Thy kingdom come, thy will be done
[TEXTO: Matthew 6:10]
We continue in Matthew 6:10: "Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven."

Here are two petitions that go together. Thy kingdom come. The kingdom of God is the place where God rules and is obeyed. Jesus said that kingdom had already arrived with him, and at the same time he taught us to ask for it to come. Both are true: the kingdom is already here, in every heart that submits to the King, and it isn't complete yet, because the world is still full of injustice. Asking thy kingdom come asks for both: that God reign today in your house, and that Christ return to reign over everything.

This was the most revolutionary petition for first-century ears. Israel was under Rome. Asking for God's kingdom to come was saying, quietly, that Caesar's kingdom wasn't the final one. The same is true today. Every time you pray thy kingdom come, you're declaring that no government, no company, no ideology has the last word over your life. It's a prayer of allegiance.

The second: thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven. How is God's will done in heaven? Instantly, gladly, without argument. Isaiah saw the seraphim and Daniel saw the angels, and none of them told God: let me think about it. That's what Jesus teaches us to ask for the earth. And the earth includes your piece of earth: your job, your marriage, your money.

There's something I need you to see here. This petition is not resignation. Many people say thy will be done with a sunken voice, like someone giving up. Jesus didn't teach it that way. Jesus taught it as an active petition: Father, make your will happen here, and use me for it. It's the prayer of a soldier asking for orders, not of a patient giving up.

Have you noticed Jesus himself prayed this line?

[TEXTO: Matthew 26:42]
In Gethsemane, Matthew 26:42: "O my Father, if this cup may not pass away from me, except I drink it, thy will be done." He prayed it in the hardest moment of his life, and he didn't pray it as defeat. He prayed it as surrender. And out of that surrender came our salvation. Pray it in your hard moment, and you're praying with the very same words he used.

And there's a practical question many people ask: how do I know God's will so I can ask for it to be done? The short answer is that most of it is already written. That you love your neighbor, that you forgive, that you're honest, that you care for your family, that you don't worry. That's God's revealed will, and you can ask for it with full confidence. For what isn't written, which job to take, whom to marry, where to live, the petition changes shape. Father, may your will be done, and give me wisdom to recognize it. James promises that wisdom to whoever asks.

So far that's three petitions and all three are for God: his name, his kingdom, his will. Only now does Jesus let us talk about ourselves. And what comes next is surprising for how small it is.

## Our daily bread
[TEXTO: Matthew 6:11]
Verse 11 says: "Give us this day our daily bread."

After asking for God's kingdom to come to the whole world, Jesus teaches us to ask for bread. Not riches, not success, not a solved life. Bread. What's needed for today. And this teaches us two things we find hard to accept.

The first: God cares about small things. The same God you just asked to bring his kingdom over the nations wants you to ask him for lunch. There is no petition too small for the Father. If it worries you, it matters to him.

[TEXTO: 1 Peter 5:7]
Peter puts it this way in 1 Peter 5:7: "Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you." All. The food, the rent, this month's bill too.

The second, and this is the hard one: daily. Jesus doesn't teach us to ask for the month's bread or the year's. He teaches us to ask for today's. That reminds us of the manna in the wilderness: God gave it every morning, and if anyone kept some for the next day, it bred worms. God wanted his people to depend on him every day, not once a year.

[B-ROLL: fresh bread, a simple table with plates]
We want long-term security. We want to know the bread for the next twenty years is guaranteed. And Jesus teaches us to ask for today's. Not because planning is bad, Proverbs is full of calls to plan, but because security doesn't come from a full barn. It comes from the Father who gives every morning.

There's a story in Luke 12 that shows the opposite danger. A rich man has a harvest so big he decides to tear down his barns and build bigger ones. And he says to himself: soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry. And God says to him: thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee. The man's problem wasn't having barns. It was that he put his rest in the barn and not in the Father. Asking for daily bread is the vaccine against that.

And why our bread and not my bread? The plural again. You can't ask for our bread on a full stomach while your brother has nothing to eat and you know it. The petition commits you. If God answers by giving you more, part of that answer is for you to share. The church in Acts took this literally: nobody had need, because those who had more put it in common.

A question for you: when was the last time you asked God for something as small as today's food? If it's been a long time, maybe it isn't because you have extra faith, but because you have an extra-full pantry. This petition is there to remind you where what's in it comes from.

## Forgive us as we forgive
[TEXTO: Matthew 6:12]
Verse 12: "And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors."

This is the only petition Jesus adds a comment to after finishing the prayer.

[TEXTO: Matthew 6:14-15]
In Matthew 6:14-15 he says: "For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you: But if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." Of the six petitions, it's the only one Jesus goes back to explain. That tells us how serious it is.

First, the word debts. Matthew uses a money word. Sin is a debt: something we owe and can't pay. Luke, in his version, uses sins directly. Both words are true. We sin and that leaves us owing.

Second, the as. Forgive us as we forgive. This has frightened many people, and understandably, because it sounds like God's forgiveness depends on ours. Is that so? Not exactly. God's forgiveness is by grace; Ephesians 2 leaves that crystal clear. But forgiveness received and forgiveness given are so bound together they can't be separated. Whoever truly understood how much they were forgiven, forgives. And whoever doesn't forgive shows they didn't understand what they received.

Jesus illustrates it in Matthew 18 with a frightening parable. A servant owes the king ten thousand talents, an impossible sum, more than he'd earn in several lifetimes. The king forgives it all. That servant goes out and finds a fellow servant who owes him a hundred pence, about three months' wages. He grabs him by the throat and has him thrown in prison. When the king finds out, he says: O thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all that debt, because thou desiredst me. Shouldest not thou also have had compassion on thy fellowservant? The parable doesn't say the king's forgiveness was fake. It says the servant never understood it, and that showed in how he treated the other man.

So this petition has an edge. Every time you pray it, you're saying: Father, treat me the way I treat those who owe me. If there's someone you haven't forgiven, this line will hurt. And Jesus put it there so it would hurt, because the pain pushes you to resolve it.

Now, I want to clarify something, because this gets used to wound wounded people. Forgiving isn't saying what they did to you was fine. It isn't pretending it didn't happen. It isn't necessarily trusting again or returning to the same relationship. Forgiving is releasing the debt: giving up collecting it yourself, and leaving it in the hands of the just Judge. Joseph forgave his brothers and embraced them, but first he tested them for months to see if they had changed. Forgiveness and wisdom go together.

Did a name come to mind while I was talking? Don't chase it away. That's exactly the name this petition wants you to bring to the Father today.

## Lead us not into temptation
[TEXTO: Matthew 6:13]
And verse 13: "And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil: For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen."

This petition has a difficulty we have to face: does God lead anyone into temptation?

[TEXTO: James 1:13]
James 1:13 says no: "Let no man say when he is tempted, I am tempted of God: for God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man." So what are we asking?

The word translated temptation also means trial. It's the same word James uses a few verses earlier.

[TEXTO: James 1:2]
James 1:2 says: "My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations." God does allow trials; he did with Abraham, with Job, with Jesus himself in the wilderness. What we ask here is: Father, don't take me to a place where I'm going to fall. You know my weakness better than I do; don't put me where I won't hold up. It's the prayer of someone who doesn't think they're strong.

Peter thought he was strong the night before he denied Jesus. He said that even if everyone else abandoned him, he wouldn't.

[TEXTO: Matthew 26:41]
Jesus answered him, in Matthew 26:41: "Watch and pray, that ye enter not into temptation: the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." Peter didn't pray, and he fell asleep. And a few hours later he fell. The petition Jesus had taught him months earlier was exactly the one he needed that night, and he didn't use it.

And the second half: deliver us from evil. The Greek can be read as evil or as the evil one. Both readings are possible and both are true. There is a personal enemy, and there is evil in the world and in us. We ask the Father to get us out of there. This is the humblest petition in the whole prayer. It's admitting we can't handle even our own temptations alone.

Notice the two halves of the petition cover both directions of danger. Lead us not: that we don't go in. Deliver us: that if we're already inside, we get out. Jesus knew that sometimes we pray before falling and sometimes we pray from the floor. Both prayers fit here.

The prayer ends with praise: thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory. Some ancient manuscripts don't have it, which is why some Bibles put it in brackets, but the church has prayed it from the beginning and it makes complete sense. We begin with Father and end with King. We begin asking that his name be hallowed and end declaring that the glory is his. The prayer is a circle that begins and ends in God, and in the middle fits our whole life: the bread, the debts, the temptations.

## How to pray it this week
Today we saw that the Lord's Prayer has six petitions. Three for God: his name, his kingdom, his will. Three for us: bread, forgiveness, protection. And all of it wrapped between Father at the start and King at the end.

The big idea today is this: the Lord's Prayer is not a prayer to repeat; it is the map of everything Jesus wants us to ask for.

And here's what I want to suggest for this week. Each morning, take the Lord's Prayer and pray it line by line, but stop at each one to put your own content into it. At hallowed be thy name, name the place where today your life can speak well or badly of God. At thy kingdom come, name the specific situation where you want God to rule today. At our daily bread, ask for what you actually need today, without shame. When you reach forgive us as we forgive, say the name that came to mind a while ago. And at lead us not into temptation, name your weakness. Seven mornings. You'll discover that the best-known prayer in the world was the one you had prayed least.

If this episode helped you, share this with someone who stopped praying because they don't know what to say. Jesus already gave them the words.

In the next episode we're going to look at Jesus' prayer in Gethsemane. He himself prayed thy will be done in the worst moment of his life. We're going to learn what to do when what we ask and what God wants don't line up.

Remember: the Lord's Prayer isn't for repeating; it's the map of what Jesus wants you to ask for. Let's pray. Our Father which art in heaven, hallowed be thy name in the life of every person who listened today. Thy kingdom come to their homes and thy will be done in their decisions this week. Give them their daily bread, teach them to forgive as they were forgiven, and don't let them fall where they are weak. For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen.
