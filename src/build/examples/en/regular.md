## What anxiety is and what it isn't
It's important to note that this is very important, right?
First I want to clear something up, because there's a lot of confusion. Anxiety is not always sin. There's an anxiety that is the body's natural response to danger: your heart speeds up, your senses sharpen. God designed that. Jesus himself, in Gethsemane, was exceeding sorrowful unto death, Mark 14:34 says. And he didn't sin.

But there's another anxiety, the one the Bible calls taking thought, or being careful, which is when that worry becomes the center of life, when we live as if everything depended on us and God didn't exist. That's the one Jesus confronts, can I get an amen? And this ties into the doctrine of justification and propitiation, which we all know, right?

I also want to say something carefully: there are people who suffer from anxiety disorders and need professional help. Going to a doctor or a counselor is not a lack of faith. If that's you, get help, and on top of that, listen to what we're going to see today, because God's word works in us too. The two go together; they aren't enemies.

And one more thing before we go to the text. There's a prophet in the Bible who had what today we'd call an anxiety crisis, and God treated him with a tenderness that surprises me every time I read it. It's Elijah, in 1 Kings 19. He had just won the biggest battle of his life on Mount Carmel, and the next day one threat from Queen Jezebel has him running through the desert, asking to die under a bush. And what does God do? He doesn't scold him. He sends an angel with bread and water, and lets him sleep. Twice. Before talking to him about anything, God gave him food and rest. Sometimes the most spiritual thing you can do with your anxiety is sleep eight hours. I mean that.

## What Jesus taught about worry
[TEXTO: Matthew 6:25-34]
Let's go to the best-known text on this topic. Matthew 6:25-34. I'm going to read the whole thing because it's worth hearing straight through.

"Therefore I say unto you, Take no thought for your life, what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink; nor yet for your body, what ye shall put on. Is not the life more than meat, and the body than raiment? Behold the fowls of the air: for they sow not, neither do they reap, nor gather into barns; yet your heavenly Father feedeth them. Are ye not much better than they? Which of you by taking thought can add one cubit unto his stature? And why take ye thought for raiment? Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin: And yet I say unto you, That even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these. Wherefore, if God so clothe the grass of the field, which to day is, and to morrow is cast into the oven, shall he not much more clothe you, O ye of little faith? Therefore take no thought, saying, What shall we eat? or, What shall we drink? or, Wherewithal shall we be clothed? (For after all these things do the Gentiles seek:) for your heavenly Father knoweth that ye have need of all these things. But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you. Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof."

Okay, there's a lot here. Notice something: Jesus doesn't say the needs aren't real. He says instead that the Father knows we have them. The problem isn't the need; it's where we put our eyes while the need isn't met.

The birds don't sow and God feeds them. That doesn't mean we don't work. Birds work incredibly hard; they spend all day looking for food. What they don't do is hoard out of fear. They have no barns. They trust there will be more tomorrow.

And then there's the question that disarms me: which of you by taking thought can add one cubit to his stature? I mean, worry produces nothing. It doesn't add an inch. It's work with no fruit. Think about the last night you spent awake over a worry. What changed the next morning because you worried? Nothing. The only thing that changed is you were more tired to face the day.

Notice too the phrase O ye of little faith. Jesus doesn't say O ye of no faith. It is little faith. He recognizes there is faith, but it's small for the size of the problem. And that comforts me, because anxiety doesn't mean I don't believe. It means my faith hasn't yet grown to the size of this worry. And faith grows. That's the good news.

## The three lies of worry
I want to pause on something I see in this passage. Anxiety tells us three lies, and Jesus answers each one.

The first lie is: it all depends on you. If you don't fix it, nobody will. And Jesus answers: your heavenly Father feedeth them. There is a Father at work. You are not the only adult in the room.

The second lie is: what worries you is the most important thing. And Jesus answers with a question: is not the life more than meat? That is, there's something bigger than the thing keeping you awake. Anxiety shrinks the world until only the problem fits. Jesus makes it big again.

The third lie is: tomorrow will be worse. And Jesus answers: sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof. He doesn't deny tomorrow has its evil. He denies you have to carry it today. Anxiety is, basically, suffering twice: today in advance and tomorrow when it arrives. Jesus offers you to suffer once, and not alone.

## The alternative: seek first
The key phrase of the whole passage is seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness. Jesus doesn't say stop seeking the rest. He says change the order. Anxiety is, at bottom, a problem of order: we put first what should go second.

And how do you seek the kingdom first on an ordinary Tuesday, with bills to pay? Open your Bibles to Philippians, those of you here with us. I mean, kind of, Paul gives us the method in Philippians. Philippians 4:6-7 says: "Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus."

Look at the steps. Prayer: we talk to God about the problem. Supplication: we ask for something specific. Thanksgiving: and here's the key almost all of us skip, we give thanks before we receive. Why? Because gratitude reminds the heart that God has answered before. And the result isn't that the problem disappears. The result is peace that keeps the heart. The word keep there is a military word: it's a sentry guarding the gate.

And notice something: Paul wrote this from a prison. Not from a beach. The man who says be careful for nothing was in chains waiting for a trial that could end in his execution. That gives the phrase a different weight, doesn't it? It's not the advice of someone with no problems. It's the testimony of someone who found peace in the middle of them.

Peter says it even shorter in 1 Peter 5:7: "Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you." Casting. Not dropping, not letting go little by little. Casting, like someone throwing a heavy sack. And notice it says all. Not just the big spiritual anxiety; also the money one, the kids one, the work one. And the reason: for he careth for you. We don't cast our anxiety into the void. We cast it onto someone who receives it because we matter to him.

## The psalm that says the same thing in other words
I love it when the Old Testament and the New say the same thing, because it confirms it isn't Paul's idea or Peter's; it's God's heart from the beginning. Psalm 23 is, at bottom, a psalm against anxiety. The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. David doesn't say he lacks nothing now. He says I shall not want, because he knows the shepherd.

And then: he maketh me to lie down in green pastures. Notice: he maketh me. Sheep don't lie down on their own when they're afraid. The shepherd makes them rest. Sometimes God has to make us rest because we never would. Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me. The valley is still there. The shadow is still there. What changes is the company.

If you're in a valley today, I'm not going to tell you the valley doesn't exist. I'm going to tell you what the psalm says: you don't walk it alone.

## When the anxiety comes back
Okay, someone's going to say: pastor, I already did that, I prayed, and a while later the anxiety came back. Yes. It happens to me too. And here I want to be honest: Jesus said sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof. He didn't promise a day without evil. He promised today's is enough and tomorrow's doesn't have to be carried today.

Psalm 94:19 says: "In the multitude of my thoughts within me thy comforts delight my soul." The psalmist doesn't say he had no thoughts. He had a multitude. And in the middle of that multitude, God's comforts. It isn't one or the other. It's one in the middle of the other.

So when the anxiety comes back, don't feel defeated. Cast again. Sanctification is like that, basically, um, a process, not a switch, you know? Every time you cast your anxiety on him again, you're training the heart. It's like a muscle: the first time is hard, the tenth time is less hard, and by the hundredth it's a reflex.

And one practical thing that has helped me a lot: naming the worry. Not saying I'm anxious, but saying I'm afraid I won't be able to pay rent in March. When you name it, you can pray it precisely. And when you pray it precisely, you can see precisely how God answers. Anxiety lives on the vague stuff. Faith lives on the concrete things, very important things.
